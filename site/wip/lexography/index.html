Lexography.

A living atlas of words.

LIVE
Guiding question
What lives beyond a definition?
Year
2026
Form
Interactive word atlas
For
People curious about language and culture

Language passes through us.

Where did the world we inhabit today come from? Not just its objects and places, but the ideas, beliefs, and meanings through which we understand it.

I kept returning to language.

Words connect people and thoughts across distances and generations. We inherit them, borrow them, change their meanings, and pass them on. Some survive for centuries, travelling through cultures and circumstances far removed from where they were first spoken.

I sometimes think of language as a virus and of us as its hosts. It moves through us, changes as it travels, and persists beyond any individual. Of course, we shape it too.

That relationship made me curious about the lives of words.

How far had they travelled? How long had their meanings survived? What had changed along the way? And what traces of those journeys could we still find?

Lexography began as an experiment in making those connections visible.

Following a word.

Consider something as familiar as coffee.

The word connects forms found in Arabic, Ottoman Turkish, Italian, Dutch, and English. Each carries linguistic and historical context, reflecting encounters between people and cultures.

A dictionary can tell us what coffee means today. Its history opens a different set of questions.

Where did these forms come from? Which meanings travelled with them? When did new uses appear? What can historical records establish about their relationships?

I wanted to give readers a way to move through these questions, rather than simply present a finished explanation.

The map offers a geographical perspective. A sequence of word forms provides another way to follow the journey. Selecting a form reveals more about its language, meanings, and historical context.

But making a journey visible introduces a problem of its own.

A line is not the whole story.

A line drawn between two places can suggest a clear direction. A sequence of words can imply an orderly progression.

Historical language change is rarely so straightforward.

In the Coffee investigation, some borrowing relationships are well documented, while others have competing explanations. Sources disagree about particular routes and dates. The earliest recorded use of a word is not necessarily the moment it originated or entered a language.

Even the earliest Arabic evidence for the coffee-related meaning remains unresolved.

I didn't want the clarity of the interface to create certainty that the research couldn't support.

That tension informs the different ways Lexography presents an investigation.

Journey offers a readable introduction, connecting word forms with geographical context.

Meaning examines what was retained, extended, or changed as words acquired new uses.

Lineage allows readers to inspect relationships between forms, including qualified and alternative borrowing accounts.

Evidence connects individual claims to their sources, passages, interpretations, and remaining uncertainties.

Each perspective reveals something different. None is sufficient on its own.

The challenge is to make them work together without asking readers to understand every complication before they can begin exploring.

Making room for what we don't know.

One of the most important distinctions I've encountered is the difference between a source and what we conclude from it.

A historical record might establish that a particular word was used in a certain context. It doesn't necessarily establish where the word originated, when it was first spoken, or every route through which it travelled.

Those distinctions matter.

In Lexography, readers can inspect the evidence behind a claim, see how a source has been interpreted, and encounter uncertainties that remain unresolved.

The intention is not to remove uncertainty from the story, but to make it understandable.

Sometimes the most honest result of an investigation is that several explanations remain possible.

An atlas still taking shape.

Lexography is a live, ongoing experiment.

For now, I've placed a limit on expanding the collection beyond 200 word journeys, focusing instead on enriching the existing investigations with more historical context, meanings, relationships, and evidence.

A larger collection would provide more words to explore. But I want to understand how much a single journey can reveal before simply increasing their number.

There are still questions about how much information an interface should reveal at once, how to represent competing histories, and how to keep a complex investigation approachable.

And there's a larger question behind all of this.

A word's history can offer traces of cultural exchange, but it cannot tell us everything about where an idea originated or how people understood it.

What might those traces reveal when we follow them carefully? And what should we be willing to leave open?

That's what I want to keep exploring.
