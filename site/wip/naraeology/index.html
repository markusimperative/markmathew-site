Naraeology.

An atlas of narrative realities.

PROTOTYPE

I’m developing Naraeology to investigate how stories form, travel, transform, and become part of the worlds people inhabit.

Research project · 2026

Where it began

I started with the Trojan legends and later accounts of how Rome and Britain came to be. The story of an ancient war could become part of a people’s explanation of where they came from. A legendary ancestor could lend a ruler legitimacy.

The same character might return centuries later with a different significance. I wanted a way to follow what had changed and ask what the surviving sources could tell me about those connections.

In what reality?

In this atlas, everything is true. The question is: in what reality?

What a story claims, how a community receives it, and what it changes in the world are different questions. Each calls for its own evidence.

Following a story

The first proof of concept begins at Troy and follows a route through its reuse in Roman and British origin stories and later Arthurian retellings.

A map locates places. A timeline situates different tellings. A lineage shows what an account may draw on. I need all three to ask what a single line between two points leaves out.

Who changes a story?

A new telling can draw on several predecessors. A narrator might retain a detail, drop it, or combine it with another tradition. The choices we can see in a telling and the motives we can establish for them are separate matters.

The people who shape a telling and the people who pass it on can play different roles. An audience also brings its own memories and beliefs, which can give a story a meaning its narrator never intended.

When does a story happen?

The age in which Arthur’s story is set and the age in which Geoffrey of Monmouth tells it are centuries apart. Arthur then takes on new meanings for audiences living centuries later.

I’m exploring how to represent a story’s own time alongside the times it was written, encountered, or reinterpreted.

Building the atlas

The prototype brings maps, timelines, lineages, reality modes, audience reception, and source records into one place. It has made me ask two different questions about every connection. What does a source show about each story? What does it show about the link between them?

An unfinished investigation

How do I show several possible ancestors or conflicting readings while making the uncertainty visible? What evidence would show that a story changed how an audience understood itself?

I expect the framework to change as I test these questions. For now, the prototype is where I try the ideas and find out what needs rethinking.
